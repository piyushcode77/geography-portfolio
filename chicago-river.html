How Chicago made its river flow the other way

A public-health crisis turned a drainage divide into an engineering problem.

AI-generated practice research pack prepared for personal study and review. Not commissioned work or evidence of independent authorship.

Opening question / story hook

A city gets its drinking water from a lake. Its waste flows toward the very same lake. What if engineers could change the direction of the river instead?

Evidence and source register

The problem

Lake Michigan supplied Chicago's drinking water. Waste discharged into the Chicago River threatened that source.

MWRD: history of water protection ↗

The earlier attempt

Illinois' official drainage history records a reversal in 1871, well before the major canal opened. The 1900 project was not the first attempt to alter the flow.

Illinois DNR: drainage history ↗

The engineering solution

The Chicago Sanitary and Ship Canal established a route that carried wastewater away from Lake Michigan in 1900. The district also constructed control works at Lockport.

MWRD: river reversal and Lockport ↗

The historical record

The Illinois State Archives preserves a photograph and historical account of the reversal, making the event suitable for a source-led historical visual sequence.

Illinois State Archives: reversal photograph ↗

The trade-off

Diverting sewage did not eliminate pollution; it moved the problem downstream. The transition from diversion to treatment is a separate chapter of urban environmental engineering.

Research approach

Trace the public-health problem and canal solution through the responsible water authority, compare chronology with the state drainage history, then consult archival and peer-reviewed records to identify the downstream trade-off.

Accuracy notes

Do not claim that every branch of the river always flows backward, that reversal alone made water safe, or that it instantly ended waterborne disease. Avoid an exact opening day because historical accounts may distinguish different construction milestones.
